# The Tidewater Archive

## One — The Cannery

The tide charts were taped to the cannery wall in three rows, and every morning Ines Marsh put her palm against the bottom row and felt the paper's damp breath against her skin, and every morning the number printed beside that day had grown by a hand's width.

Nine centimeters by October. Eleven by December. Fourteen by the first thaw.

She had stopped needing to look. The water told her where it was by sound: the flat slap under the loading dock at low tide, the sucking kiss of it against the seawall at the turn, and at night, when the wind came off the Atlantic and pushed the whole bay up against the town like a hand against a chest, a low continuous knock against the cannery's foundation, patient, rhythmic, as if something out there were asking to be let in and was willing to wait years for the answer.

"The numbers don't lie," Osip said from the doorway of the cold room, though nobody had asked him anything. He said it most mornings. He was seventy-one years old and had the habit of narrating his own thoughts as though the room were a class that had fallen asleep.

"The numbers never lie," Ines agreed, "and they never tell you anything either."

"That's the difference between a number and a person." He shuffled past her with a mug of tea that smelled like burnt toast. "A number you can trust. A person you have to decide about every single day."

The Cannery had been an actual cannery once, sardines mostly, and then a boat repair shed, and then nine years of nothing at all, wet and hollow and full of pigeons. Then the Reach Historical Trust had rented it for a dollar a year and moved in the shelves and the drying cabinets and the reel machines, and hung a laminated sign by the door that said THE TIDEWATER ARCHIVE — PRESERVING THE VOICES OF KAPLAN'S REACH, and then the Trust had run out of money, and then almost everyone had moved away, and now it was Ines and Osip and forty-one thousand magnetic reels of other people's memories, and a bay that was coming up the street.

She lifted the day's first cartridge from the intake box. The label was written in blue ballpoint, in the careful block capitals of someone who did not write often: *ELLEN T. PERRY — 1974–1998 — CHILDREN, HOUSE, THE BOAT, DO NOT KEEP THE PART ABOUT MY SISTER.*

Every reel had a note like that. People had recorded themselves on the Ledger — that squat gray home console with its single amber eye and its two spindles, sold for eleven years in the nineties and then discontinued so completely that the company's manuals were now worth more than the machines — and on the tapes they had said the things they could not say aloud on a Tuesday. Then they had died, or moved, or forgotten, and the reels had gone into drawers and attics and shoeboxes, and now the trust was paid to preserve them before the sea took the drawers and the attics and the shoeboxes together.

Ines fed the tape through the cleaning gate, set the heads, and closed the lid. The machine whirred. On the monitor, a woman in a kitchen described her children's hands, one at a time, age by age, as though inventorying a house she expected to leave.

At ten o'clock the front door banged open and the water came in first, an inch of it, brown and quick, followed by Finch Alderman hauling a milk crate against his chest.

"Door's open," he announced.

"The door is a door. It opens. That's the point of it." Ines took the crate from him because his arms were shaking. He was eleven, or twelve — he'd told her both, at different times, and she had decided not to press — with the flat guarded face of a kid who had learned early that adults ask questions as a way of finding out what you've done.

"Got a haul," he said. "Ninth Street, the blue house. They left the door unlocked so I went in because technically it's abandoned now, Deirdre said abandoned houses are municipal, and I found these under the stair."

He unloaded: a bulb of black glass; a shoebox of photographs swollen into a single paper brick; a brass key on a string; and the Ledger, intact, its amber eye dull and dead, its casing scarred with salt.

Ines turned the machine over. The serial plate was rusted but the model number survived. MK-4. The last of the line, the one with the twin record heads.

"Does it work?" Finch asked.

"Does the sea work." She set it on the bench. "It might. The heads are probably ruined. Everything this close to the water is ruined eventually; that's the whole business model."

"You're sad today."

"I'm always like this."

"No, you're not." He was looking at her the way children look at adults they've decided to be loyal to, which is worse than being looked at by anyone else, because it can't be argued with. "You're like this when you've been at your mom's."

Ines put the shoebox of photographs on the shelf of things to be documented. "Bring me the reels," she said. "If there are reels."

There were reels. Four of them, in the crate's false bottom, and they were wrong.

She knew the feel of a Ledger reel the way a cook knows the weight of a good knife. All four of these were heavier than they should have been, and the hubs were brass rather than plastic, and instead of a label each one carried a single letter written in wax pencil on the flange: *A. C. C.*

And the fourth one had no letter at all. It had a date, in wax pencil, faded but legible — *AUG. 1999* — and beneath it, in the same hand, a name that Ines read twice and then a third time without any of the three readings making sense, because the name was not on any list she had ever been given, and because it was, in a way she could not have explained to Osip or to Finch or to anyone, a name she already knew.

*MARIAM.*

Outside, the wind came off the water and the cannery's foundation knocked, patient and rhythmic, like something out of the deep asking to be let in.

## Two — The Cold Room

The cold room was the only part of the cannery that had ever been properly built, because it had once held twenty tons of fish and the men who poured its floor had understood, in a way the Trust's volunteers never did, that some things must simply be kept from the air.

Osip kept it at fifty degrees and fourteen percent relative humidity, which was, he claimed, exactly the climate of a good funeral. Inside, the reels lived on steel shelving in acid-free boxes, each box with a card, each card with a number, and the number linked to a line in the intake ledger where Ines wrote what the donor had said about the tape and what she had heard when she played it and what she thought it was actually about, in a column that had no official name.

Osip called that column the guess column. He had told her once, early, that a good reel-keeper spends most of her time guessing, because the tape is never the truth; the tape is a thing a person decided to say.

He was at the bench with the MK-4 opened like a beetle when she came in with the four reels in a towel.

"I need you," she said.

"You need my machines. Different proposition." He wiped his hands on his shirt and looked at the reels and did not say anything for a long moment, which from Osip was roughly equivalent to a shout.

"Brass hubs," he said at last.

"You've seen them."

"I've seen one. Once. Nineteen ninety-eight." He picked up the reel marked *A* and turned it in the light so the tape's edge caught and flashed. "The MK-4 had two record heads. That was the whole feature, that was what the brochure sold. You could record two voices at once on one tape, which people used for interviews and, you know. Arguments. But there was a defect."

"There was a defect."

"There was a defect that the company knew about and never announced, because announcing it would have meant recalling eleven years of product. If two MK-4s were recording within about forty meters of each other — a thin wall, two rooms of one house, a pair of houses on a small lot, which in this town is every pair of houses — the fields bled. Not much. A few seconds here and there. But a *few seconds here and there* is all a memory is, Ines. It's a few seconds here and there, and then a story you built on top of them."

She put her hand flat on the cold bench. "Cross-writing."

"That's what the repair bulletin called it, in the one memo that ever used the word. Every other document called it *field interference*, which sounds like weather." He set the reel down. "Where did the boy find these."

"Blue house, Ninth Street. Under the stair."

Osip's mouth did something small. He went to the shelf of loose cards, the ones for donors who had never been documented, and pulled a card that had been in that drawer since before Ines worked there. He put it on the bench between them.

*KAPLAN, R. — 12 NINTH STREET — 4 REELS — DONATED 2003 — DO NOT DIGITIZE. — O.V.*

"That's your handwriting," she said.

"That's my handwriting." He looked at the reels the way a man looks at a dog he'd hoped had died somewhere else. "Ruth Kaplan was my neighbor for thirty-one years. She gave me these in 2003 and she told me, in her kitchen, with the door shut, that they weren't hers and she didn't want them copied and she didn't want them played, and she wanted them to go into the ground with her. Then she died, and I kept them, because I'm a coward and because I couldn't stand to throw away a voice." He picked up the reel marked *MARIAM* and held it out to Ines. "Now the water's brought them back up. You want my guess?"

"I want your guess."

"The sea doesn't give things back," Osip said. "It only ever asks a second time."

They set the reel on the deck. The MK-4's heads were corroded but they had cleaned, and Osip aligned them by feel, the way he did everything, whistling two notes through his teeth. Ines threaded the brass-hubbed tape onto the take-up spindle and felt it resist.

The monitor woke to static. Then a hiss. Then, under the hiss, the low room-sound of an old house in summer: a screen door, a fan, cicadas laid down like a floor.

Then a child's voice, very close to the microphone, so close it was almost inside Ines's own ear, saying:

*"Are you on? Are you on? Say something if you're on."*

And then a woman's voice, warm and far away and achingly familiar, saying: *"I'm on, love. I'm always on when you're talking."*

Ines sat down on the cold room floor, and outside the water knocked, and for eleven seconds she did not breathe, because she knew that voice. She had heard it every day of her life. It was her mother's.

She knew it. She was certain.

And she had never once, in twenty-seven years, heard her mother say the word *love.*

## Three — Cora

Cora Marsh lived in the house on Pemberton Street that her mother had lived in, and her grandmother before that, a narrow two-story with a porch that had been rebuilt twice and a front step that had been rebuilt never, so that getting inside required a small negotiated hop over a place where the wood had gone soft as bread.

The house was two streets up from the water line as drawn on the Withdrawal maps, which meant it was outside the mandatory zone and inside the *encouraged* zone, which meant a man in a lanyard had been to the door three times with a folder of averages and a tone of careful optimism, and Cora had put the kettle on every time, and served him tea, and asked him about his children, and then declined.

Ines came in the side door at half past five with groceries, and found her mother standing in the middle of the kitchen holding a can of tomatoes at arm's length, studying the label as though it were a difficult letter.

"Hullo," Ines said.

"I don't know where this goes," Cora said.

"In the cupboard by the stove. Where it's always gone."

"Ah." Cora put it there carefully, adjusting its face so the label showed, and then she turned around and her eyes came into focus and she said, "Ines. You're here," in a voice that meant she had been somewhere else for a while and had only just got back, and that she was not going to say anything about the trip.

They made dinner. Cora peeled potatoes at the sink and talked about the neighbor's fence and the price of scallops and a program about penguins she had watched that afternoon, and Ines answered all of it, and watched, as she always watched, for the places where the conversation's floor gave way.

There was a map on the wall of the hall, an old one, the whole bay in green and yellow like a bruise healing. Cora had marked on it in pencil the houses of everyone who had left in the last four years, small careful x's, until the land side of the map looked like a page of arithmetic. She had never once marked her own house. Ines had asked her about it exactly one time.

"He's very patient, the man with the folder," Cora said, carrying the plates. "I like him. He thinks I'm stubborn."

"He thinks you're the last one."

"He thinks I'm the last one, yes." Cora sat down. "That's different from thinking I'm stubborn. Stubborn is a compliment a man gives a woman when he's run out of arguments. The last one is what you are when the argument is over and you've still got your coat on."

After dinner, Ines said it. She had planned to say it in the morning, at the table, with coffee, in the calmest voice she owned. Instead she said it over the washing-up, holding a plate, looking at the window, which was, she suspected, how it was always going to go.

"There's a recording. On one of the reels Finch brought in. It's a woman and a child, and it's — the woman sounds like you."

The dish went still in Cora's hands.

Ines kept her eyes on the window. "The reel's dated August 1999. It's labeled with a name. Mariam." She made herself say it plainly. "I don't know what it is. I thought maybe it was yours, and you'd want it kept, or wanted it not kept, or —"

"Don't play it." Cora's voice had gone flat and quick, a voice Ines had heard maybe four times in her life, and never about anything that mattered at all. She set the plate in the rack with exaggerated care. "Those tapes lie, Ines. That's the thing nobody says. Everybody's so worried about losing the past. Nobody warns you they'll be lied to by it."

"What lies?"

"All of it. Machines." Cora dried her hands on the towel, folding it in thirds, then in thirds again. "I've been forgetting things. You don't have to look at me like that, I know what I've been doing. Tuesday I put the milk in the broom cupboard. Last month I stood at the bottom of the stairs for eleven minutes trying to remember which way I was going. And do you know what I've noticed? Every time a thing goes, it doesn't leave a hole. Something fills it. I remember a red bicycle that I never had. I remember my father taking me to see the icehouses, except my father never took me anywhere, he worked. My head is full of strangers' furniture."

She looked at her daughter and for a moment her face was entirely awake, entirely her own, and Ines, who had spent eight years learning to read the other readings of that face, felt afraid of this one, because it was the face of a woman who has decided something.

"If you record me," Cora said, "you will get one of the fillers. You will get a red bicycle and you will believe it, and you will keep it, and someday you'll be old and you'll tell your children about the red bicycle, and there will be one more lie in the world with the authority of a machine behind it. That's all you do in that cannery, love. You manufacture authority."

There it was again. *Love.* Thrown off at the end of a sentence like a coin dropped in passing. Ines heard the word and felt the cold-room floor under her again, and the reel turning, and her mother's voice saying *I'm always on when you're talking* to a child in a house by the water.

"Where did Gannet House go?" Ines asked.

It was a shot in the dark, taken because the phrase had been in her head all afternoon without any source. She had assumed it came from the reel. She kept her eyes on her mother's face to see whether it had come from somewhere else.

Cora's hands found the towel again. She wiped a dry counter with it, twice, and set it down, and said, "We're not doing this tonight," and went up the stairs one at a time, holding the rail, counting the treads under her breath, seven, eight, nine.

Ines stood in the kitchen a long while. Then she went to the hall and looked at the map on the wall with all its pencil x's, and she took down the tin box of pencils from the shelf, and she looked at the water line inked in blue along the bottom edge of the bay, and she did not do anything with either.

## Four — The Reel

She played it three times that night, in the dark, with the little lamp off, listening with her hands.

First pass, the shape. A house in summer. Cicadas. A screen door on a spring, and the spring had a squeak that rose in pitch, so the door was coming in or going out; you could map the whole day by the squeak and the fan and the long nothing in the middle, which was the sound of afternoon in a place with water nearby.

A girl, small, six or seven, who did not understand that the machine was recording and therefore said everything twice: *Say something if you're on. Are you on now? Are you on?* A woman moving in and out of range, setting the table, humming, calling the girl in for lunch, washing something, and once — this was the part that Ines kept coming back for — saying, in a low voice, clearly not to the girl, apparently to nobody at all, apparently just out of her own chest while standing at a window with the water moving beyond it: *"I'll tell you the truth when you're older and I hope by then I'll still know it."*

Second pass, the seams. Here was where it got strange. Because twice, three times, the tape jumped. And in the jumps the room changed: the hum of the refrigerator going down half a tone and coming back; the cicadas cutting out mid-note and returning at a different distance, as though the house had been picked up and set down in a slightly different part of the same summer; and once, for six seconds, the sound of a second microphone, faint and ahead of the first, in which a different woman was saying a different thing, the words smeared together like two people speaking in an adjoining room.

Cross-written. Osip's bled fields. Two houses, two machines, one tape. Ines laid her palm flat on the cold bench beside the MK-4 and thought about how a memory works. Not the recording — the memory. How you get one thing and put another thing next to it, and the tape, which does not care about you, keeps both.

Third pass, she listened only to the girl.

Because the girl was the trouble. The girl had a way of ending sentences, a little push-down on the last word, that Ines recognized. She recognized it because she had a photograph at home, on the shelf, of herself at seven, standing by a fence with her mouth open, saying something she would never know, and she had looked at that photograph for years and thought: *that's how you talk when you think you're being listened to.*

The girl said: *"You have to say the whole year. You have to say the year so it's true."*

The woman said: *"Nineteen ninety-nine."*

The girl said: *"Say my whole name."*

And the woman laughed, warm, far, and said: *"Mariam Rose Alderman, you are a very small tyrant."*

And the tape ran on, and the girl said, in the absurdly formal voice of a child who has been given a task she thinks is important, the last words on the reel:

*"If you find this, you have to bring it back to Gannet House. Because that's where I left the rest of it. Ask for the well."*

Then: the squeak of the spring, and fifteen minutes of water, and the tape ran off the reel with a soft flapping sound that made Ines jump, in the dark, alone in a cannery that was one street from the sea.

Mariam Rose Alderman.

She sat with the name for a long time. She got the intake ledger down from the shelf and turned to the back where the old names were, the pre-digitization donors, the ones entered by hand in Osip's small square writing, and she read every page of it standing up, with a pen in her hand she wasn't using, and there was no Alderman anywhere in that book, in any column, under any spelling.

Which meant nothing, by itself. Half the town had never been in the book. The book was only who had come to the cannery.

But it was the name of a child in the year 1999, and the machine in the drawer had been Ruth Kaplan's, and Ruth Kaplan had been Osip's neighbor for thirty-one years on Ninth Street, which was three hundred meters from Gannet House if Gannet House was where the reel said it was, which was on the low green spit that everyone in Kaplan's Reach called the Neck, and which on the Withdrawal maps had gone from *encouraged* to *mandatory* in March.

Ines turned off the lamp. She got the storm coat off the hook and the flashlight off the bench and she stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame, listening to the water knock and knock, and she thought: *she knows*. Not *she might know*. Not *maybe*. Her mother had heard the name Gannet House in a kitchen and had dried an already-dry counter with a towel and had gone up the stairs counting the treads, and that was the sound of a woman holding a door shut from the inside while someone kicks it.

She went out into the dark, and the water, ankle deep on Fourth Street, was warm.

## Five — The List

Deirdre Okonjo worked out of a trailer behind the old fire station, and the trailer had a sign on the door that said WITHDRAWAL COORDINATION, REACH COUNTY, and under that, in smaller letters, PREPAREDNESS IS CARE.

Ines had been inside it four times in three years and had never once gotten used to the smell, which was new carpet and burnt coffee and the particular cold nothing of a room where bad news is kept in folders.

"Marsh," Deirdre said, without looking up. "Two things. Your mother."

"She's not signing."

"She's not signing, and she's the last residential address inside the encouraged zone on the north side, and the county has stopped pretending the road stays passable after the spring. So she's not signing, and in April we stop maintaining the road, and after that the nice young man with the folder stops visiting, because I won't send anyone down a road we've stopped maintaining." Deirdre finally looked up. She had a face built for this work, calm and unarguable, and Ines liked her, which was inconvenient. "That's not a threat. I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm telling you the calendar, because you're the only family and you should have it."

"Give me the calendar."

"Last bus is the second of May. After that it's arranged transport only, and arranged transport only runs for medical and for the archive, which brings me to the second thing." She pulled a folder. "Your building."

"The cannery's outside the line."

"The cannery was outside the line. The line was redrawn in June on the new surge modeling, and the cannery is now inside the ninety-five percent envelope for a hundred-year event, which in this new world means a bad Wednesday." She turned the page around. Ines looked at the map of her own workplace with a blue line drawn through it, and through the loading dock, and through the cold room by about a meter and a half.

"You have four options," Deirdre said. "One: the trust relocates the archive inland; they've had an offer of a warehouse in Farrow, sixty kilometers, it's dry, it has a roof, and it has no budget for staff, which means the reels sit in boxes in the dark for a decade. Two: the county takes custody and the collection goes to the state repository, same story, longer drive. Three: we lift the whole cold room onto a flatbed in one piece in May, which is expensive and which the county will not pay for. Four: you digitize what matters and leave the tape."

"The tape is the thing. A digitized reel isn't a reel."

"I know," Deirdre said, and for a moment the two of them looked at each other across the trailer, two women who had both chosen to spend their working lives on the losing side of a long argument. "I know. That's why I'm telling you in February and not in April."

Ines took the folder. Then she said, in a voice she kept as level as she could, "I need the residential index. Pre-2000, north side, all addresses, everyone who's been moved since the program started."

"Why?"

"Names."

"For the collection."

"For the collection."

Deirdre looked at her for four seconds — long enough that Ines felt the lie settle in the room and be tolerated — and then opened a drawer and took out a bound printout six centimeters thick and dropped it on the desk with a sound like a book of scripture.

"You didn't get it from me. It's the 1998 assessment roll, the last complete one, plus the program's relocation list." She put her hand flat on it. "Ines. If you're looking for something that was buried, be careful. This town had a habit of burying things under other things, and the water's been taking the top layer off for eleven years now. It's a bad season to go digging."

-----

It took her three evenings.

She did it at the cannery, after hours, with the door locked, because she did not want Osip to see her doing it, and she told herself this was because he'd ask questions, and knew, by the third evening, that it was because she was afraid of the answer he'd give.

The 1998 roll: every property, every rateable occupant. She went down the Neck. There were fourteen addresses on the Neck in 1998. Nine of them were the same families in both lists. Four were seasonal, non-resident. And one, the last one, the one at the end of the spit where the water was already eating the road in 1998 —

*LOT 14, NECK ROAD. OCCUPANT: GANNET HOUSE TRUST. RATEABLE VALUE 0. RESIDENTIAL USE, UNOCCUPIED.*

Unoccupied. A house with a name and no people.

She went to the relocation list and read it backwards, which was the way the names made sense, because it was alphabetical by family and she didn't know what she was looking for. Alderman. Aldridge. Bancroft.

There.

*ALDERMAN, J. & M. — LOT 14 NECK ROAD — GANNET HOUSE — DECEASED, 09/1999 — DEPENDENTS: ONE (F). — TRANSFERRED TO GUARDIANSHIP OF KINSHIP COURT, REACH COUNTY, 10/1999 — FILE 99-3114.*

One female dependent. Guardianship, October 1999.

Ines sat very still in the cannery with her hand on the page, and the water knocked under the floor, and she thought about the way her own name appeared in no list anywhere in this town: not in the roll, because Cora rented for years before she bought; not in the school records, because the school's records before 2004 had been in the basement that flooded in '04; not in the county's files, because her birth certificate was a late registration stamped in November 1999 with a hospital name that had closed in 2001.

Late registration. November. She had seen the certificate. It was in the tin box in her mother's hall, under the pencils.

She turned off the machine. She put on her coat. She walked out into the street, where the water had risen another hand's width since morning, and stood in it, feeling how warm it was for February, and said out loud, to nobody, in a voice she barely recognized as hers:

"File 99-3114."

Then she went home, because the alternative was to go to Pemberton Street and stand in her mother's kitchen at midnight and ask, and she was not ready to hear the answer out loud in the house where she had grown up, in the room where her mother had served tea to the man with the folder, and declined.

## Six — The Neck

The road out to the Neck was a single lane of failed asphalt with beach grass growing up its center, and it had been closed since March, and the barrier at its mouth was a sawhorse with a county sign on it, and the sign had been pushed into the ditch.

Ines drove past the sawhorse at seven in the morning, in Osip's truck, because she did not want her own car out there, and she could not have said what difference she thought that made, and she did not examine it.

The Neck was two kilometers of low green land shaped like a thumb, and by 1998 the sea had already taken the first joint. She drove with the water on both sides. Behind her, the town receded into a line of roofs and one church spire and the cannery's flat gray hat. Ahead, the spit narrowed and the grass gave way to sand and the sand gave way to what was left of a stone wall that had once pretended to be a seawall and was now a row of grey teeth.

Gannet House was not there.

She got out of the truck and stood in the wet grass and looked at it, and the looking took a long time because there was so little to look at. A foundation of split granite, roughly twenty meters square, standing about forty centimeters above the sand, with dune grass and rosa rugosa grown up through the middle of it. A chimney base on the north side, taller, because chimneys survive, and the brick had been burnt black on its inside face, which did not happen in a house that fell down.

Fire. That was the fire.

Around the foundation, scattered in the grass, the ordinary archaeology of a summer place: a rusted milk can, a length of chain, a hand pump gone to a stub, three bricks, the pelvis of a plastic lawn chair, and, at the southeast corner, half-buried in sand, a length of cast iron pipe with a wooden cover over it, standing upright, with the cover nailed down.

The well.

*Ask for the well.*

Ines stood over it for a full minute before touching it. She was aware, in a way that made her feel foolish and eleven years old, of being watched — not by a person, but by the house's absence, which is a kind of presence and which every reel-keeper in that town learned to feel. Then she knelt down and worked the cover up. The nails had been put in by hand and they came out of the wood easily, which meant the wood was soft, which meant it had been wet for years, and the cover came away and she looked down into a hole with a black stone lip and nothing visible inside it at any depth.

She went back to the truck for the flashlight and a length of rope, and lowered the light into the shaft on the end of the rope.

The well was about four meters deep and dry, and the bottom was sand and debris and one object that was not debris: a metal strongbox, powder-blue, with a hasp, sitting on the sand at the bottom of a well on a spit of land in the middle of a bay that had been reclaiming the ground for eleven years, dry as a biscuit.

It took her twenty minutes to get it up, with the rope around the hasp and her feet braced on the lip and the wind coming off the bay hard enough to make her eyes water, and when it came over the edge she sat down in the grass with it in her lap and had to put her head down for a moment, because the ground out there did not feel like ground.

The hasp was rusted; the box wasn't. She got it open with the tire iron. Inside, wrapped in a piece of oilcloth that had done its job perfectly for more than twenty years, were three things.

A reel, brass-hubbed, wax pencil on the flange reading *C. — JAN. 1999*.

A tape cassette of the ordinary audio kind, in a clear case, labeled in a different hand, a young man's, block capitals gone loose at the ends: *GANNET HOUSE — LAST WEEK — DON'T LET THEM SAY IT WAS THE WIRING.*

And a photograph, small, square, color gone to orange, of a house with a porch and a screen door and a gable window, and below it, standing on the step, a woman with her hand on the shoulder of a small girl in a yellow bathing suit, and the woman was younger than Ines had ever known her, twenty years or more stripped out of her face, but it was her. Cora. Standing in front of Gannet House with a child who was not Ines, in the summer of 1999, in a photograph that no one had ever shown her, holding onto a girl whose name she was now nearly certain she knew.

Ines sat in the grass with the wind off the water and the light going flat and gray, and she understood two things at once, in the way you understand things at the Neck, which is the way you understand things in dreams: that she had been lied to by the person she loved most in the world, and that the lie had been built to hold something up.

She put the box in the truck, and the photo in her inside pocket, and she drove back past the fallen sawhorse with the water on both sides, and the whole way home she did not once look in the rearview mirror, because she knew what was behind her and it was a house that wasn't there.

## Seven — The Ledger Mechanics

Osip was asleep in the chair by the drying cabinet when she came in with the strongbox, which is to say he had his eyes closed and his hands folded on his chest and the radio on low, and he came awake the way old men do, all at once and entirely, without any of the slow surfacing that belongs to the young.

"You went to the Neck," he said.

"You knew where it was."

"Everybody knows where the Neck is. I've been looking at it from my kitchen window for forty years."

She put the strongbox on the bench between them and opened it. He looked at the reel. He looked at the cassette. He did not look at the photograph, which she had not taken out, which told her something, because a man who doesn't look at a thing has decided in advance that the thing is going to look back.

"C.," he said. "January ninety-nine."

"You've seen this before."

"No." He rubbed his face. "No. But I know what it is, because Ruth told me in 2003 what she'd been keeping, and I've spent twenty-two years hoping she was exaggerating, which was a stupid thing to hope, because Ruth never exaggerated. Ruth was the least fanciful woman I ever met." He turned the reel over. "Do you want to do this properly, or do you want to do it fast?"

"Properly."

"Then I'm going to tell you how the machines worked, because you've been guessing, and if you're going to break a town open, you should do it with the right tools."

He went into the cold room and came back with two MK-4s on a hand truck, one of which was the archive's working unit and one of which was the unit from the blue house, cleaned and re-headed in the last week by Osip's own hands, and he set them a meter apart on the bench and plugged them in.

"Two heads," he said. "That's the whole trick. Every other home recorder has one record head and one erase head. The MK-4 had two record heads, so you could lay down two tracks and get a stereo image, or you could mic a room and get a room. And because the heads were this close" — he tapped the casing — "the erase gap of head one sat inside the record field of head two. That's the defect. Not in the tape. In the geometry."

"Meaning what."

"Meaning that when the erase head passes over the tape, it doesn't only erase what's on the tape. It emits a field. And the other machine's record head, if it's close enough, feels that field and *writes it down*. On the other machine's tape. Six, eight seconds sometimes. You'd get the other house's room, the other house's voices, printed under your own, quiet as a thought." He looked at her. "But it doesn't go one way, Ines. It goes both ways. Both machines print each other. Two houses, one afternoon, written on each other's tape forever, and neither one of them recorded anything but its own kitchen. You follow me."

"I follow you." She felt the cold of the bench through her hands. "So four reels came out of Ruth Kaplan's house. Four reels, one of which is labeled in a child's voice with the name Mariam."

"One of which is not from Ruth Kaplan's house at all," Osip said. "Ruth had three reels of her own and she gave me all four boxes because the fourth box was already in her basement when she moved in, and she never played it, and she wanted it gone, and she was too frightened of it to burn it in case it mattered. Now." He held up two fingers. "Two questions. One: where is the machine that printed it. Two: where is the machine that it printed."

"The second one is in this room."

"It is now." He set the blue-house machine down beside its twin and squared it. "And the first one is in a well, apparently, which is a thing you did this morning, which we will discuss when I have finished being impressed and started being angry."

They set up the transfer in the cold room because it was the driest place in the building and the machines were old and old machines, like old men, work best in cold air. Osip ran head-cleaning tape through both units, first one, then the other, and he did it with the attention of a man vesting something at a church.

"Now," he said. "Here's the part I should tell you before we start, and then you can decide, and if you decide wrong I will still make the tea."

"Tell me."

He put down the cleaning tape. He looked at the reel from the well, at its wax pencil, at its *C. — JAN. 1999*, and Ines saw, for the first time in the eight years she had known him, that Osip Vaughn was close to something like tears, which on that face looked like nothing more than tiredness.

"I had a brother," he said. "Dima. Three years younger. He was on the Neck during the fire, because he was a volunteer, because in this town everybody was a volunteer, because when the call went out at two in the morning on the eleventh of September 1999 about nine people went out onto the spit in the dark in a pickup truck with a pump that nobody had serviced."

"Osip."

"The house burned to the foundation in forty minutes. Gannet House was a summer house with a balloon frame and old wiring and it had been closed up and reopened every year since the war and nobody had ever fixed the wiring, and that, officially, is what happened, and I have spent twenty-two years accepting it, and my brother was thirty-four and he drowned in nineteen centimeters of water on the Neck Road when the tide came over the road behind them and the truck went off the edge." He said it very evenly. "They told me it was the wiring. Everyone was told it was the wiring. There's a stone at the church with his name and *volunteer firefighter* under it and I have looked at that stone maybe three hundred times."

He put his hand on the blue-house machine, the one that had come out of a dead man's stairwell nine days ago.

"And a cassette came out of that well this morning that says *don't let them say it was the wiring*," Osip said. "So I'm going to ask you to play it, and I'm going to ask you to play it slowly, and I want you to know that whichever way it goes, I am not going to be able to be the reasonable one in this building anymore. That job's yours now."

Ines reached over and put her hand on his arm, which she had never done, and he let her, which he had never let anybody do, and they sat like that in the cold room, two people in a temperature built for fish, while outside the bay knocked and knocked at the foundation of the cannery, asking.

## Eight — Finch

Finch Alderman turned up at the cannery two days later with a crab in a bucket and the news that Deirdre's people had started painting numbers on the doors of Ninth Street.

"Numbers for what?" Ines asked.

"For the inventory. So they know what to lift." He set the bucket on the bench and looked into it. "It's dead. It was already dead, I didn't do it."

"Finch."

"It was already dead." He looked up, and there it was, the subject he had come to talk about, sitting behind his eyes like a coin in a pocket. "The blue house got a number. Fourteen. And I went back because I left my — it doesn't matter. I went back and there was a woman there, from the state, in the same coat as the folder man, and she asked me what my name was and I said Alderman, and she wrote it down, and then she looked at the paper a lot longer than she needed to."

Ines put down the reel she was cleaning. The cannery was cold around them and the water was up to the loading dock with the tide coming in, and she stood there and decided something in about four seconds, and afterwards she thought that was probably how every bad decision of her life had been made.

"Come in the cold room," she said. "And get the stool. And whatever I say to you, you don't tell anyone else, because if you tell anyone else you'll be telling a story that isn't finished, and unfinished stories hurt people, that's all they do."

She told him. Not all of it, but the shape of it: the four reels, Ruth Kaplan's basement, the rice of brass hubs, the girl on the tape named Mariam Rose Alderman. She watched his face while she said the name, because she had spent a week telling herself that *Alderman* meant nothing, that it was a common name, that in a county of eleven thousand people the clerk at the town office had three Aldermans in one drawer of a filing cabinet.

Finch's face did absolutely nothing. That was the tell. A twelve-year-old who has never heard a name looks confused, or bored, or asks. A twelve-year-old who has heard a name at home, in a certain tone of voice, over a certain table, goes still and careful and starts arranging his own hands.

"I don't know any Mariam," he said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I was going to." She sat down across from him. "Who is she, Finch."

He picked the dead crab up by one claw and put it down again. "Every year on the twelfth of September my grandmother goes out to the Neck and stands on the road because she can't walk on the sand anymore, and she puts flowers down right where the road starts, and my mom hates it, they fight about it, they have fought about it every year of my life. And there's a frame on the wall in the front room with three pictures, and two of them are a man and a woman that nobody talks about, and the third one is a baby in a yellow bathing suit, and one time I asked whose baby that was, and my grandmother said *ours*, and my mother came into the room and said *Mom* in a voice like a door slamming, and that's the whole of what I know."

Ines sat with that.

"What's your grandmother's name?"

"Ruth."

The room went very quiet, and then very loud, because that's what happens when a thing you have been circling for a week simply arrives. Ruth. Ruth Kaplan of Ninth Street, who had bought a house in 1961 with a husband who had died in 1974 and who had lived in it fifty-one years with three reels of her own and a fourth she was too frightened to burn, Ruth Kaplan who walked to church on Sundays, whose grandson was sitting on a stool in the cold room of the Tidewater Archive with a dead crab in a bucket.

"Kaplan was her married name," Ines said slowly. "Your mother's maiden name, or —"

"My mom's name is Kaplan-Alderman. She kept both. She said once it was for the paperwork." Finch's voice had started to go narrow and fast. "What's going on. Why do you look like that. You know something, you've got the face on, this is the face you had when you found the —"

"Finch."

"*What's going on?*"

And Ines, who had been lying for a week and who was very tired, and who was looking at a boy whose grandmother had kept a dead man's record under a stair for twenty-two years, and whose family had a baby in a yellow bathing suit on their wall with the word *ours* under it, did the thing she had been afraid of doing since she found the reel.

She got up and put the reel marked *MARIAM* on the deck, and threaded it, and turned the volume down low, and pressed play.

A screen door squeaked. Cicadas laid themselves down like a floor. A small girl's voice, coming out of the speaker into the cold air of a cannery, said: *Are you on? Are you on?* And a woman's voice, warm, far away, said: *I'm on, love. I'm always on when you're talking.*

Finch slid off the stool. He didn't cry or yell or ask questions. He got up and walked to the bench and stood with both hands flat on the wood, the way you stand at the edge of very cold water, and he listened to fourteen seconds of a child telling a woman to say her whole name, and then Ines stopped the tape because she did not think the boy should hear the part about the well from a machine.

"That's her," Finch said. "That's the picture."

"I think so."

"She's a baby in the picture. In the picture she's a baby."

"The reel is from 1999. She'd be —" Ines did the arithmetic and did not like where it went. "My age. My exact age, more or less."

Finch looked at her. He was twelve, or eleven, and he had the flat guarded face of a kid who had learned early that questions are how adults find out what you've done, and he looked at Ines with that face for a long moment and then let it go, and underneath it was somebody much younger, and much more frightened, and very much in need of a task.

"What do we do," he said.

Ines looked at the water on the loading dock, ten centimeters below the boards, and at the tide chart in its three rows, and at the boy, and at the reel.

"We find out what happened," she said. "And then we decide what to keep."

## Nine — What the Cassette Said

They played it that night, the three of them: Ines, Osip, and Finch, who had been told to go home and had gone home and had come back at nine with a thermos of his grandmother's tea and a look on his face that made it clear he was going to be in the room.

The cassette was ordinary cheap ferric tape, forty-five minutes a side, recorded on a handheld deck, the kind a young man in 1999 might own if he worked with his hands and liked machines. Side A began with eight seconds of pocket noise, and then a man's voice, young, breathing a little hard, saying very quietly:

*"Okay. It's about one in the morning, September the eleventh. I'm standing on the Neck Road with the pump truck. And I want to say first that this is my own tape and nobody asked me to make it and I'll say so if anyone asks."*

Osip had his hands folded on his chest and his eyes closed. Ines watched his face. It did not move at all, which meant he was listening as hard as a person can.

*"The call came in at one forty-seven. Structure fire, Gannet House, Neck Road. Because the house is — I mean was — at the end of the road and the road was already going, they sent one truck, which is what the manual says for a non-residential structure outside the main district. Me and Wallace and Pete. Wallace drove. We got onto the spit at about two twenty because the road was under and we were going slow, and the water was maybe ten centimeters. Warm. It's the bay, it's always warm in September."*

*"House was well involved when we got there. Whole south side going, upstairs windows gone. And here's what I have to say, and if I get it wrong somebody should correct me, because I'm going to write it down and read it back."*

A pause. Wind. The pump running. Then, more slowly, the way a man talks when he is doing two things at once and wants to be accurate about the one that isn't the fire:

*"There was a car in the yard. Not a truck, a car, small, dark, forty meters up the road from the house where the grass is gone. It had been there long enough that the windshield was fogged inside. When we came up the road the car pulled out and went past us going the other way and it went onto the grass, onto the shoulder, and it went *steady*, all the way, and Wallace said something about tourists and I looked at the plate and I don't remember the plate."*

*"And I want to say that the wiring thing is wrong, and I want to say why. Because the power to Gannet House was shut off in 1994 when the trust that owned it stopped paying, and the meter was pulled, and I know that because I pulled it, me and Dima Vaughn pulled that meter in October of ninety-four, and there was no power in that house to start a fire. It was a kerosene house. Everybody knew it was a kerosene house."*

Osip's hands came apart on his chest and one of them found the edge of the bench.

*"Two forty-five, we're getting water on the north side and the roof goes and it's gone. Two fifty-one, the tide comes over the road behind us at maybe thirty centimeters and the pump truck starts to float, and Wallace yells to get out, and we're walking out, and Dima — Dima went back for the deck. Because there was a deck gun on the deck, on the second hose, and he went back for it because it was county property and he was like that, and the road was gone by then, and — "*

The tape ran on. The man on it did not say anything for eleven seconds. Then he said, in a voice that had gone entirely flat, the voice of a young man deciding to finish a sentence because he had started it:

*"And I couldn't get to him because I couldn't swim. I'm fifty meters away and it's not even deep and I can't swim and there's nothing to hold onto. That's the whole thing. That's the whole of it."*

*"If anyone finds this: the house was empty that night. Nobody was in it. I checked. I went around the outside before we put water on and I checked the ground floor windows and there was nobody in it, and I have thought about that a hundred times since, and I have decided that the reason I checked is that I already knew there might be someone in it."*

Then the pocket noise, and the click, and side A ended.

Osip got up. He went out the back door of the cannery without his coat and stood on the loading dock in the dark with his back to them, looking at the water, which was four centimeters under the boards by then, slapping at the joists. Ines and Finch sat in the cold room and listened to the machine hum and did not say anything to each other for a while, because there was nothing you could say to a man learning that his brother had died for a piece of county equipment, or, worse, learning that there had never been any need for him to be out there at all.

When Osip came back in his hands were steady. He had brought, from somewhere, the three reels from Ruth Kaplan's own box, which he had been keeping in the safe since 2003 and had never played.

"She told me once," he said, "that there were things about that night she'd put down and didn't want said while she was alive. Well, she's dead eleven years and I'm seventy-one and my brother's been dead longer than she was alive when she said it to me. Sit down."

Ruth Kaplan's reels were nothing like the others. She had made them in 1999, one a month through the autumn, and she had made them the way a woman files a report. She identified herself at the start of each. She gave the date. She spoke in sentences with ends on them, and Osip sat with his hand over his mouth the whole time.

From the second reel, October 1999:

*"My name is Ruth Kaplan, forty-one Ninth Street. I am making this because I am the only person in this town who will say it aloud and I want it said once, by a person, on a machine, so that someday when the water takes the houses someone can check me."*

*"The Aldermans had a summer in Gannet House from 1996. Husband was John, wife was Marya, she was from away, she had a way about her, people called her proud and she wasn't proud, she was foreign. They had a little girl. Mariam. Four years old in ninety-nine, and she spent the whole summer out there with them and the whole summer before, and Marya was pregnant that summer with a second and it did not go well and she lost it in July and after that she got quiet."*

*"Now. There was a person who came to that house in the last week of August, and it was not a stranger, and it was not the husband. It was known to them and it was a woman and she came three times and the third time there was an argument that could be heard down the road and my window was open and I heard two sentences of it and one of them was 'you cannot take her.'"*

Ines had stopped breathing.

*"On the eleventh of September, in the night, John Alderman died in that house. He was in it, that's the thing the county says, that's the record, he was in it and it burned and there was no insurance and no inquest because there was no power to start it and nobody wanted to know what did. And Marya Alderman was dead before that, she was dead in July, whatever the file says, I don't know what happened to her but I know she was not in that house in September and I know the child was not in that house in September, because the child was in mine."*

*"The little girl slept in my back bedroom for six weeks from the seventh of September to the middle of October and then the court came and took her away and put her with a family and I was told I was too old and too ill and I was told not to talk about it and I was told there was a file and the file was sealed for the child's protection."*

*"I want whoever hears this to write down the date: the seventh of September, 1999. She was four. She had a yellow bathing suit in a bag and one photograph of her mother and she cried for her father for a month and she called the woman who came for her by a name she had for her, a name that was not mother but was something, and I am not going to write it down here because I am afraid of it. There. That's said."*

And then, because Ruth Kaplan was a woman who filed reports, the last thing on the third reel, recorded in December of 1999, with the sea already over the Neck Road every spring tide:

*"I have one more thing to say and then I am done. In October a woman came to my door. She asked me for the child's things. She was very calm and very polite and she had been crying. She asked me to say nothing to anyone, and she gave me a reason, and the reason was a good one, and I have kept it to myself all these years and I am going to keep it. But I want on the tape that she asked me, and that I said no, and then I said yes. Because that's the truth of what I did. I said no first."*

The reel ran off the hub.

Finch said, in a small voice, "That's my grandmother."

"That's your grandmother," Ines said.

"She's on the wall in the front room, the picture of the baby, she's got it up on the wall where she sits." He was crying now, without any drama, tears coming down his face in a way that made him look much younger and much angrier. "She's been keeping that picture on the wall for twenty-two years and everybody in the house knows and nobody says it. Everyone in my house knows about the baby on the wall."

Osip said, quietly, "Everyone in this town knows about a thing. That's what a town is, son. It's a group of people all holding the same stone."

Ines stood up and got her coat, and the two of them looked at her — an old man and a boy, in a cold room, at midnight, with the water coming up under the floorboards.

"Where are you going," Osip said.

"To my mother's house," Ines said. "Because there's one thing on that tape, and it's the reason she asked your neighbor to say nothing, and I already know what it is, and I need her to hear me say it to her face."

## Ten — The Lucid Hour

She let herself in with her own key at half past midnight and found her mother awake and sitting in the front room with the light on, fully dressed, with her coat over the arm of the chair and her shoes on and the tin box on her lap.

"I've been waiting," Cora said.

"For me?"

"For whoever came." She had the tin box open: birth certificate, a lease, an insurance paper, three keys. "I've been sitting here since eight. I knew it would be tonight, because I heard the truck go past on the Neck Road yesterday and there's only one truck in this town that goes out there, and Ines, love, I want you to do something for me before you say anything, which is to sit down and let me talk while I still have it."

Ines sat.

The lucid hours were a thing she had learned about from a nurse two years before, and had been waiting for ever since, and there had been four of them, and each one had lasted somewhere between twenty minutes and an hour, and each one had been a gift she was now required to spend at speed. Her mother's eyes were entirely awake. Her hands were steady on the box.

"I was thirty-one," Cora said. "In 1999. I taught the fourth grade at the Reach school and I had a flat on Ferry Street and I had a life that was perfectly ordinary and I want that written down somewhere, because it's the last thing that was true about me before this started."

"Before what started."

"Before the file." She took a breath. "I knew Marya Alderman. She was from Gdańsk, she was the cleverest woman in this town by a distance and she had no friends, and she had me. I was at Gannet House all that summer, I was there nearly every weekend, I taught Mariam to swim off the end of the dock. And in July Marya lost the baby and went somewhere in herself that I could not reach, and in August she asked me to take the child."

Ines sat absolutely still.

"She was not well, Ines. She was not in her right mind and she knew it, and she said to me, standing at that kitchen window, *you cannot leave her here with him*, and I said to her, *I will not take a child from her father*, and I said those words to her, and I have said them to myself every day for twenty-seven years. That's what Ruth meant. *I said no first.*"

"The fire."

"The fire, and here is the whole of it, and then I'm going to stop, because after this I can't promise anything." Cora looked at the wall, not at her daughter. "On the tenth of September John Alderman came to the school at four o'clock in the afternoon and he told me his wife was gone, and he told me he could not raise the child alone, and he told me he would not give her up, and he told me he had told her mother's people in Poland that the child had died with her, and I have thought about that afternoon every day. And I did not go to the police, because there was nothing to go to the police about, because a man is allowed to keep his child, and because I was thirty-one and frightened of making a scene, and because the woman who asked me for help was by then in a hospital in Boston under a name I did not know."

"Mom."

"On the night of the eleventh there was a fire in that house and I was not there and I did not set it and I have never known who did, and I have made myself believe it was the kerosene because the alternative is a thing I cannot carry." She said all of this without a tremor. "What I did, after, is this. I went to the Aldermans' lawyer and I went to the court and I told them the child's mother was dead and the child's father was dead and that the child's maternal family were in Poland and unreachable, and that the child had no one, and that I would take her, and the court gave me a guardianship in October and I renamed her. Mariam Rose became Ines, because Marya's mother was called Ines and I thought, I still think, that it was the only name left of her mother that I could give her."

She looked at her daughter then. Full on.

"And I came to Ruth Kaplan's door in her own house and I asked her to say nothing, and I gave her a reason, and the reason was good."

Ines could barely get the words out. "What reason."

"That if anyone in this town knew who she was, the file would come back, and the file would say the child of a woman who was committed and a man who died in a fire, and that child would be an Alderman, and an Alderman in this county gets nothing. No guardianship, no house, no school records, no name, and I would have no claim and she would go to the state, and I would never see her again, and I could not — " For the first time, her voice moved. "I could not do it. I told Ruth that I was the only person who had said no, and that I would spend my whole life making the no into a yes, and I asked her to let me."

Cora set the tin box on the floor. She took off her shoes and put them side by side under the chair, the way she did every night, because that was the way she had been taught and it was one of the things the disease had not taken.

"I have been forgetting for four years, Ines. And I have known the whole time that there would come an hour when I could not tell it, and that I would need someone else to hold it. And I have spent the last four years hiding it from you because I was afraid of how you would look at me, and that was wrong, because the hiding is what turned the truth into a lie, and that's a sin I did by myself, nobody made me."

She stood up. She was swaying slightly, and the awake look was beginning to go out of her face the way a light goes out in a window of a house you are walking past, not all at once, but in a room at a time.

"So," she said. "Now you know what I did. And now I'd like to sit down, because I'm very tired, and tomorrow I won't have it, and you're going to have to be the one who says it."

Ines got up and put her arms around her mother, and stood in the front room of the Pemberton Street house holding a woman who had renamed her out of the only thing a frightened person has left, and stayed there while the tide under the town came up over the seawall and into the street, and thought, with absolute clarity, the thought she would keep for the rest of her life.

*The reel is the only one of us that will still be able to say it.*

## Eleven — The Recording

It took her five weeks to ask, because she had to be certain of the answer herself before she could bear any answer her mother gave.

Cora signed the Withdrawal papers on the eleventh of March, in the trailer behind the fire station, with Deirdre Okonjo as witness and a cup of the bad coffee in front of her, and she signed them with her own name and then looked at the pen for a while. "Cora Marsh," she said. "That's the last of me that's on paper. Everything else in this town has me as somebody's mother."

Then, in the cannery, on the twentieth of March, in the cold room, with the door shut and the machines warm, Ines asked her.

"I want to record you. Not to keep, if you don't want it kept. To say it. Once. Into a machine, in a room, with me there, and then it exists, and it can't be filled up with a red bicycle."

Cora sat on the stool and looked at the two MK-4s on the bench with their lids open, and at the boy Finch, who had been allowed to stay because he had earned it, and at the reel marked *MARIAM*, which Ines had brought out and set down where her mother could see it.

"Sixty-six years old and I've never once said the whole thing out loud," Cora said. "Except to you, in the dark, that one night, and you were the person I did it for, so it doesn't count the same."

"It counts the same."

"Then yes," Cora said. "But I want the boy to know why, first. Boy. Finch. Come here." She took his hands, both of them, in hers. "Your grandmother said no to me first and then she said yes, and I want you to understand what that means. It means that when I knocked on her door she did the right thing, and I asked her not to. That's the whole shape of it. Anything you hear on that tape that sounds like blame, hand it to me and I'll carry it, because I'm the one who asked."

Finch said, "Okay."

"Say it again so I know you mean it."

"I mean it."

Osip set the levels. Ines threaded a new reel, brass hubs, and marked the flange in wax pencil: *C.M. — MAR. 20XX — WHOLE ACCOUNT — KEEP.* Then she started the machine, and her mother spoke into the microphone for forty-one minutes, and said, in her own voice, at a distance of about thirty centimeters from a small brass-hubbed wheel that would spin for longer than anyone in that room would live:

*"My name is Cora Alice Marsh and I am sixty-six years old and I was born in this town. On the seventh of September 1999 I took a four-year-old child out of Gannet House on the Neck Road, with her mother's consent and without her father's, and on the twenty-first of October of that year a court made me her guardian and I gave her a new name. I have told no one this for twenty-seven years."*

And then everything.

When the machine clicked off, the four of them sat in the cold room for a while. The tide was coming up in the street and the cannery's foundation was knocking, slow and patient. Then Cora reached over — quite steadily, quite soberly, watchful — and pressed *record* again, and leaned in close to the microphone, and said:

*"Addendum. To be fair to the machine. I did not tell her because I was afraid, and I was afraid because I loved her, and that is not an excuse and it is also not nothing. Both of these things are on the tape now. Whoever finds this: I was not a good person. I was a person who got the whole of what she wanted and paid for it in a currency only one other human being had to spend, and it was paid, and she's sitting next to me."*

Finch, whose grandmother was at home holding a photograph of a baby in a yellow bathing suit, wiped his face with his sleeve and said to nobody in particular, "I'm going to go and get her. Can I go and get her."

"Go and get her," Ines said.

## Twelve — The Withdrawal

Ruth Kaplan came to the cannery three days later, on a Tuesday, walking with a stick that she did not need, in a coat the color of wet stone, and she stood in the cold room for eleven minutes looking at the shelves, and then she said to Osip, "You kept them."

"I kept them."

"Twenty-two years."

"Twenty-two years."

"Well." She adjusted her grip on the stick. "Then I'd like to make a statement, and I'd like it on tape, and I'd like it done properly, because I have been reading for twenty-two years and I have never once been able to say the whole of it."

They recorded her that afternoon, for fifty-five minutes, and she did not need the notes she had brought. She identified herself, gave the date, and did what she had done in 1999: said the thing a person says when they have decided that the keeping of a record is a form of citizenship. Then she sat with Cora in the drying room, two old women who had been neighbors across a kitchen for two decades and had never once said aloud the thing that joined them, and after an hour Ines went in to bring tea and found them both asleep in their chairs, her mother's hand on the older woman's sleeve.

The water came into the cannery's loading dock on the second of April. It took the east street on the ninth. On the eighteenth, a county crew lifted the intake cabinet and the drying racks onto a flatbed and drove them to Farrow, sixty kilometers inland, to a warehouse with a roof and no staff, and the Trust's director came down from the state capital for two hours and made a speech about stewardship, and Osip did not attend.

Because they had made another decision first, the four of them, in the cold room, over three evenings and a great deal of tea. Not to digitize everything. To digitize the ordinary: nine thousand reels of kitchens and children and arguments and boats and Christmas mornings and a man explaining how to file a tax return, all of it onto drives that would go inland in Osip's truck, and which any institution in the country could host, because a drive is a fact and facts travel.

And the cold room — the actual reels, the tape, the thing that is not a fact but a voice — they did not send to Farrow. They packed it into the waterproofed basement of the church, four meters above the new surge line, in acid-free boxes in a room with a dehumidifier and a sump pump and a key that Deirdre Okonjo had arranged, quietly, in a county file marked *INFRASTRUCTURE — HERITAGE, SECONDARY*, which no one would ever read.

Forty-one thousand reels, in a church basement, on a hill, above a town that was leaving.

Finch carried boxes for nine days and did not complain once, which worried Ines more than the complaining would have, and on the last day he asked her, in the loading dock, with the water up to their ankles, whether he could keep the crab bucket. She said yes. He took it home on the bus with the rest of his family's things in two suitcases and a milk crate, and his grandmother held the photograph of the baby in the yellow bathing suit on her lap the whole way to the transit hotel in Farrow, and when the bus went around the last bend before the bridge she turned it face down, and then turned it up again, and that was, Ines thought, the most honest thing she had seen anyone do all spring.

## Thirteen — Tidewater

Cora Marsh did not leave until the twenty-sixth of April, and she left the way she had done everything since 1999, on her own terms and one day after everyone had stopped expecting it.

She had been declining for six weeks. There were now whole days she spent in the early nineties, and she had begun to talk about someone called Marya, who she said was coming to the house soon and who liked the window open. Twice Ines found her standing in the kitchen holding a can of tomatoes at arm's length, exactly as she had in February, and twice she put it away herself, in the cupboard by the stove, where it had always gone, with the label facing out, and Ines stood in the doorway and understood that this was the shape of the rest of both their lives: a house slowly filling up with other people's furniture, and a woman who would keep putting the cans away correctly long after she had stopped being able to say why.

On the last morning, Ines woke early and found her mother already dressed, sitting on the porch step in the wet light with the tin box beside her and the door open behind her.

"It's the second of May," Cora said.

"It's the twenty-sixth of April."

"It's close enough." She was watching the street, where the water had been standing for eleven days and had stopped pretending to be a storm. "I've been sitting here since six watching the road. Forty years ago I could have told you every family on this street and what time their lights went on. Now it's four houses and a foundation."

The bus came at nine. Ines had arranged it; the arranged transport, medical and archive only, and there had been paperwork, and Deirdre had signed it without comment and told her the hotel had a kitchenette and that it did, in fact, take pets.

Cora stood up. She looked at the house for the length of four breaths. Then she took the tin box — birth certificate, lease, insurance paper, three keys — and put it in her daughter's hands and said, "That's yours now. I can't be trusted with paper," and got on the bus and chose a seat on the water side, and Ines, standing in the street with the box in her hands, watched her mother not look back.

-----

She stayed. That is the part people in Farrow found difficult to believe later, and the part she stopped explaining.

Osip went to Farrow with the drives, because somebody had to, and because he was seventy-one and his hip was bad and he had spent the winter saying that the archive was people and not reels and then failing to leave it, and in the end he left because Ines made him, and took the last intake ledger with him as a hostage so that she would have a reason to follow.

She kept the cannery through the summer, which was unreasonable, and which she did because the lease ran to October and because the county's last estimate of the date the building would be untenable had been wrong by eleven years and running. She lived upstairs in the flat where the cannery's foreman had lived in 1931, with a generator and a foot pump for water and a small boat tied to the loading dock, and she worked. She digitized the last of the north side: the Perry reels, the church choir of 1968, eleven hours of a fisherman named Aubrey Voss describing the bottom of the bay in a level of detail no chart has ever matched, a schoolteacher who recorded herself reading every book she had ever loved out loud, one entire reel of a dog barking, donated by a family who wrote on the card, *HE WAS A GOOD DOG AND HE WAS CALLED OTTO.*

Every Sunday she took the boat up the Neck, if the weather allowed it, and stood on the last of the spit and looked at the water where the well had been, which by August was under a meter of bay. And once a week she drove inland to the hotel and sat with her mother, and on the good days they walked to the end of the lot where a fence separated the building from a field, and Cora would hold the rail and look at the field and say, "The water's not right here, is it."

On the second of September she got the call from Osip, who had been organizing the drives at the warehouse in Farrow with a county archivist and Ruth Kaplan's three reels. *"We're getting requests,"* he said, on the bad line. *"From the state, from the university, from a historian at the capital. Everybody wants the fire. Everybody wants the Cora reel. And I want to ask you a question, because it's your name on the box."*

"Ask."

*"Do you want to be the girl in it, or do you want to be the archivist?"*

She stood in the cannery office with the water knocking under the floor and thought about Ruth Kaplan saying *I said no first*, and about her mother saying *I was not a good person*, and about how the tapes lied in only one direction — they could never be kind, but they could never be unfaithful either — and she said, "Both. Write both. Put my name on the card. Daughter of the donor, subject of the recording, keeper of the archive."

*"That's a long card."*

"Put it on the card, Osip."

-----

In October the last two houses on Pemberton Street came down, and the fourth street went, and the aquifer rose into the old town's basements, and the county posted the final closure notice on the Neck Road, and Ines took the notice off the post and kept it, because she had learned in February that paper is the thing a town leaves behind and someone has to hold it.

The last reel in the dock was the one she had been putting off. It had come in during the first week, in the very first intake box, before Finch, before the Neck, when she had still believed she was a woman with an ordinary job in an ordinary town. The card said: *CHILD'S VOICE — UNKNOWN FAMILY — FOUND IN WALL DURING DEMOLITION, THIRD STREET. PLEASE IDENTIFY.*

It was a brass-hubbed reel. She had never noticed, in nine months, because she had never had a reason to.

She threaded it, and set the heads, and pressed play, and a screen door squeaked on its spring, and cicadas came up off a summer that was now twenty-eight years deep, and a small girl's voice, very close to the microphone, said:

*"Are you on? Are you on? Say something if you're on."*

And Ines Marsh — who had been christened Mariam Rose Alderman in a house by the water in 1995, and renamed out of her mother's name in 1999, and who was now the last reel-keeper in a town that had been handed back to the bay on schedule, without any ceremony, the way a person hands a sleeping child back to someone else — leaned in close to the microphone, and answered.

*"I'm on,"* she said. *"I'm always on when you're talking."*

Then she boxed the reel, wrote the number on the card, entered the number in the intake ledger in her own hand with the date, and in the column that had no official name, the guess column, she wrote four words, and closed the book, and went down to the boat, and rowed home across the drowned town, over the roofs and the street signs and the school where her mother had taught the fourth grade, to the flat above the cannery, where the tide chart was taped to the wall in three rows and the water knocked at the foundation all night, patient and rhythmic, asking to be let in.

*The tape is not the truth, she had written. The tape is what we decided to say. The rest of it is the water, and the water is not sorry, and it is not cruel, and it keeps everything we do not, and somewhere under all of this, in a basement with a dehumidifier and a sump pump and a key that no one ever reads about, there are forty-one thousand voices in the dark, saying our town, over and over, in every voice we had.*
